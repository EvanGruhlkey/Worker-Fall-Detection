Rig Fall Detector Trial 1

We trained the fall classifier nine different ways and tested each one on rig footage it had never seen. This page reports how many falls each version caught, how many false alarms it raised, and what to do next.

Prototype: not ready for deployment

Best result

The best setup trains on our rig clips plus the public CAUCAFall fall dataset. On rig footage it had never seen:

13.5 of 21rig falls caught (64%), with an alert within 1.5 s of impact
0.17 smedian alert time after impact, for the falls it caught
6false alarms in 5.8 minutes of footage with no fall

What this means. The detector reliably recognizes clear trips, slips and collapses, and it alerts within a fraction of a second. It is not yet good enough to deploy, for two reasons. It misses about a third of falls, mostly because the person is not detected at all while falling. And it raises false alarms when workers lie down under pipework to do maintenance.

Both problems have a clear next step (see the end of this page).

How it was tested

Data

  • 31 rig clips (AI-generated): 21 with a fall (slips, trips, ladder and stair falls, a collapse) and 10 of normal work.
  • 100 CAUCAFall clips (public dataset, ten people indoors, day and night camera): 50 falls and 50 everyday movements like kneeling and sitting. Used for training only.
  • Every fall's start and impact were timed by hand.

Method

  • Each video becomes a stream of body keypoints (17 joints per person). The classifier watches each person's last 2.5 s and decides whether they are falling.
  • Every rig clip is scored by a model that never saw it or its scene (5-fold cross-validation).
  • Each setup was trained twice and the results averaged, which is why some counts end in .5.
  • A fall counts as caught when the alert fires for the person who fell, no later than 1.5 s after impact.

What causes the false alarms

The false alarms we inspected on the normal-work clips all came from workers lying or kneeling under pipework during maintenance. The classifier has learned that a person lying on the deck usually means a fall. It has seen too few examples of people lying down on purpose.

The alert threshold

The alert fires when the model's fall score stays above a threshold for about 0.4 s. Raising the threshold cuts false alarms without losing falls, all the way to 0.99. The model is very confident about both falls and lying workers, so the threshold alone cannot separate them. That needs better training data.

Limits of this trial

  • The rig footage is AI-generated. Results on real rig cameras may differ. A test on real footage is needed before any deployment decision.
  • The data set is small: 21 rig falls. One more or one fewer caught fall moves the rate by about 5 percentage points.
  • The false-alarm footage is short and deliberately hard: 5.8 minutes, much of it workers lying under equipment. It cannot yet show the target of under one false alarm per hour of normal work.
  • The live system still uses an older pose model than the one these results come from. It will be switched to the same pipeline before deployment.

Next steps

  1. Add footage of workers lying, kneeling and crouching on purpose: under pipes, at valves, beside pumps. This targets the main source of false alarms.
  2. Test a larger person detector (YOLO26s or YOLO26m) for falls where the worker is lost mid-fall. This raises the 18-of-21 ceiling, and it matters live as much as in training.
  3. Record and test on real rig camera footage, including staged falls onto mats, to confirm the results carry over from AI-generated video.
  4. Train the final model on all clips with the best setup, then rerun this report to compare.
